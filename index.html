Welcome

I don't know how you arrived here, but I'm glad you did.

I'm a family man, a teacher, a lover of wisdom, and a songwriter who has been carrying songs in notebooks for over twenty years. I write music as a way to fight back against the ways this world can manipulate us. I write because I see music as a stepping stone from being merely human to reaching our highest potential. I write to find the transcendental in mundane everyday life.

These songs are gifts. I don't know exactly where they come from, but I know they don't belong to me alone. I recorded them in a bedroom in one day because life won't give me more. I named this project Soulpuller because that's the job description: pulling souls toward something higher.

I want to make music that makes time seem to stop. I want to make lightbulbs turn on in people's heads. I want to nourish people's souls with wisdom. I want to make beautiful lullabies that contain vital wake-up calls buried inside.

If any of that resonates with you, stay a while. Listen. Read the lyrics. The songs are for you.

Only love,
Mark

The Story

How the songs got here

Mark Cleaver was in Manhattan, looking at the sky, when the planes hit the towers. He was twenty years old, a long way from home, and the world he thought he understood broke apart in front of him. What followed were years of darkness — the kind that doesn't announce itself, that sits quietly in the background of an ordinary life until it becomes the whole picture. He lost friends to the edge he nearly went over himself.

Somewhere in the middle of all that, he started writing songs. Not because he decided to be a musician, but because the songs arrived and wouldn't leave him alone. Fragments of lyrics sat in notebooks for years, carried from London to New York to Bath to Vienna to a city in northern Spain, composting slowly until they were ready. The writing drew from everything — philosophy, spiritual practice, the wreckage of his twenties, the slow rebuilding that followed. Each song was a way of turning darkness into something that could help someone else through their own.

He recorded them all in a single day, alone in a bedroom, one voice and one guitar, because life wouldn't give him more than that. He named the project Soulpuller because that's the job description: pulling souls toward something higher. He has essentially zero audience and has won no awards. He doesn't care. He keeps writing because the songs don't belong to him and he can't die with them trapped inside.

“I can't leave these songs to go to the grave with me unheard.”

The Interview

100 Questions with Soulpuller

In March 2026, Mark sat down for a hundred-question interview about the music, the mission, and everything in between. These are the moments that mattered most.

What does success look like for Soulpuller?
Anonymous with lots of listeners who don't know what I look like. Everyone having the lyrics on their lips and the songs in their heads. People putting it on in dark times, going to sleep, taking downtime in a quiet room. Occasionally a lyric jumps out and turns a light on.
In your own words — what is Soulpuller about?
My music is about continuing the beautiful reciprocal human arrangement that Mr. Antolini spoke about in Catcher in the Rye. It's about being troubled by the state of the world, needing hope, answers, philosophy, wisdom, things to make life bearable. When you start to make it bearable and keep building up, eventually you can make it magical and transcendental and wonderful. It's about the journey from bearable to transcendental. It's about finding the glorious, the transcendent, the jubilation, the magic, the wonder, the joy in basic daily life.
The people who have the platform are wasting the platform. What do you mean?
Music should be this amazing, incredible, uplifting, divine experience. Everything that's missing from mainstream music — it's superficial, it's bubblegum, it's throwaway. I find it frustrating, depressing. The people who have the platform don't see what music could be. They're wasting the platform.
You want musicians to be more like authors. What do you mean?
The book stands on its own, and the author can be unknown. I don't want to stand up and take attention. I think most performers are clowns. I want to reinvent the rules so that the music speaks for itself. You don't need to know what I look like. You don't need to see me on a stage. You just need the songs.
You've been carrying songs in notebooks for over twenty years. How do you know when one's ready?
Songs evolve over years. Often a lyric or a nugget of philosophical wisdom comes to me, sits in a book for years before I come back to it and see how it fits. Usually lyrics first — beginning with an experience, a feeling, something that's happened to me. Occasionally melodies come first, or a guitar riff when I'm messing around. There's no defined approach; that's just the way the magic happens. The process is more like composting than composing. You carry the fragments and wait for the right moment.
You believe your songs are gifts from somewhere beyond yourself. Do you worry that sounds mad?
It's like meditation. When you're meditating, you're connected to a higher power. The mind is pure and clean. You go into the heart and meditate on the heart, and feel the energy there. If you can remove the ego and become one with the universal soul, the universal heart, the shared consciousness — that's what I want to come out when I'm singing. The Soulpuller will be continually bringing people in so we're joining together and raising up together, raising our vibration. I don't know exactly where the songs come from, but I know they don't belong to me alone.
You're forty-five. Is it too late?
If I had been famous young, I would have died at twenty-seven, one way or another. I wouldn't have been able to cope with fame. The darkness needed processing, the philosophy needed living, the songs needed composting. Forty-five isn't late — it's ready.
Cohen didn't release his first album until thirty-three. Tom Waits peaked commercially in his forties and fifties. Rodriguez was in his seventies when the world found him. The music industry worships youth. Music listeners worship truth.
You call yourself rubbish. You also believe you can channel the universal soul. How do those two people coexist?
It's big me and little me. We all have both sides. Calling myself rubbish first means I won't be hurt if others do. But I've also got this desire to reach the highest possible states. I want the music to be the best thing anyone's ever heard. But that's not the effect it has, so it's almost like if I can't do that, then I won't do anything.
Your daughter was six when she quoted one of your lyrics back to you. What did she say?
She said, “It's like in your song when you say the mind is a wonderful servant but a terrible master.” She was six or seven. She applied it to her own life. That's the entire artistic mission proven in miniature — a child understood the philosophy because it was delivered inside a song she'd heard at bedtime.
You recorded everything in one day. Why?
All songs recorded in one day in the bedroom of my house. Guitar very quiet in the mix, voice front and centre. I tried to get into a meditative state before recording. Life schedule, not enough time, and urgency: I don't want to die and have them trapped inside me.
Who should never listen to Soulpuller?
People who are looking for numbness. People who found Leonard Cohen depressing. Soulpuller demands presence. If someone is using music as anaesthesia, this is the wrong medicine.
What would you say to your younger self?
I believe in you.
